# Ops Console v1 runs accounts, not hotels

The HospAI Ops Console that shipped on 2026-11-24 covers the account half of a vendor back office and covers it well: a registry of customers and hotels, plan and add-on changes, suspension, operator management, one-hotel health and copy setup, with every write a named command that needs a reason, lands in an append-only log and is visible to the hotel. Judged against how Cloudbeds and Mews visibly operate and against general multi-tenant SaaS practice, that is stricter than the incumbents on staff access and audit, and it already contains the parts that are expensive to retrofit. What it lacks is the operations half: a fleet-wide view of health and alerts, a controlled way for support to look inside a hotel, a console control for the kill switch, any view of queues, versions or restores, a record of where each hotel is in onboarding, and any channel to tell hotels about incidents. The order to consider the missing parts follows the serial roadmap. First, with no new slice, finish R7.2 (its alert sweep already runs on staging but pages no one, so it needs a paging sink, a status page and a production deploy), write a kill-switch procedure, run the restore drill and fix three inconsistencies in the repository. Second, when the R7.3 spec is written, put fleet health, the alert list, support grants, version tracking and a small set of user fixes into it. Third, before about ten hotels, give practice mode, tenant export, an onboarding record, announcements and queue replay their own specs. Billing (R5.6), data import (R4.8), cancel and purge, break-glass SQL and central tax rollout touch decisions the owner has already locked, so this report raises them as questions only. Two limits on the evidence apply throughout: no PMS vendor publishes a description of its internal console, so every statement about Cloudbeds and Mews is indirect, and the Indian regulatory facts come from secondary summaries that must be checked against the primary notification before anyone acts on them.

## The short answer: eight things a PMS ops console must contain

An ops console is the vendor-side back office used by the vendor's own staff. It is not the hotel-facing product. Cloud guidance calls the layer underneath it the "control plane": the services that create, configure, bill and observe every tenant (a tenant here is one customer organisation and its hotels). The research supports this short list.

1. **A tenant registry with lifecycle.** Every customer and hotel, searchable, with status, plan, creation date and lifecycle state (in setup, live, in hypercare, suspended, closing, purged), and the commands that move between states.
2. **Commercial state next to technical state.** Plan, add-ons and limits, and at least a read-only view of whether the customer is paid up, because suspension and go-live both depend on it.
3. **Separate staff identity and an action log.** Staff sign in through their own identity store with a second factor, hold named roles, give a reason for every write, and cannot alter the log. The hotel can see the part of the log that concerns it.
4. **Controlled support access into a tenant.** A time-boxed, reasoned, visible way to look at (and, with a second approver, change) a hotel's data, plus the routine user fixes: resend an invitation, unlock an account, remove a leaver.
5. **Fleet health.** One screen across all hotels: open alerts, night-audit state, backlog age, schema and code version, last contact. Queues and failed messages can be inspected and replayed.
6. **Safety switches.** Kill switches, read-only mode and per-tenant release controls, each toggled by an audited command and listed when not in its default state.
7. **Onboarding and recovery tools.** A go-live record per hotel, copy of setup from a template, an import pipeline with a dry run, a practice mode, tenant export and a rehearsed restore.
8. **Hotel-specific operations.** A night-audit board, channel and integration health, dated tax and statutory rules, and a way to tell affected hotels about an incident that does not depend on the product being up.

HospAI has items 1 and 3 in full, most of 2, one piece of 7 (copy setup), and fragments of 5. Items 4, 6 and 8 are planned, deferred or absent.

## The evidence is indirect: no vendor publishes its console

No public document from Cloudbeds, Mews, Apaleo, Oracle or Stayntouch describes an internal admin console, and searches of both help centres found nothing on impersonation, a support-access switch, session time limits or how staff sessions appear in a hotel's audit log. That absence is itself a finding, and it is the largest evidence gap in this research. What can be seen comes from three kinds of trace: contracts and data-processing agreements, help-centre phrases of the form "contact Support to...", and the third-party tools visible around the edges. Mews names Salesforce for its support desk, Okta for staff identity, LaunchDarkly for release cohorts and Atlassian Statuspage for incidents; Cloudbeds runs its help centre on Zendesk and its status page on Instatus ([Mews Platform Documentation](https://www.mews.com/en/legal/platform-documentation); [status.cloudbeds.com](https://status.cloudbeds.com/)). (Inference) Vendors of this size assemble much of the back office from commercial tools and keep a thinner in-house layer for tenant-specific actions; the public record does not show how thick that layer is.

The researchers also read many pages through a fetch tool that summarises, so short quoted phrases in this report should be re-opened at the source before being reused as verbatim quotes. Facts seen only in a search-result summary are marked "(search summary)". Statements marked "(Inference)" are reasoning from the documented facts, not vendor documentation. The general-practice sources are strongest where they are primary (Microsoft's Azure Architecture Center, AWS, the PCI DSS v4.0.1 text, Cloudflare's developer documentation, identity-vendor product docs) and weakest on "what to build first", where the writers are mostly vendors and consultancies. SOC 2 and ISO 27001 points come from compliance vendors' summaries, not the standards themselves.

## HospAI's first version is strict where the incumbents are vague

**Staff access is the clearest case.** The Cloudbeds contract lets the company access customer data to provide the service, to address technical problems, or at the customer's request for support; the Mews terms contain no clause on support access at all, and its data-processing agreement only limits access to personnel performing services ([Cloudbeds Master Terms](https://www.cloudbeds.com/master-terms-2026/); [Mews DPA](https://www.mews.com/en/legal/data-processing-addendum)). The model at both is purpose limitation, with no per-session consent and no published customer-visible record. The few concrete traces show tiering inside the vendor: Cloudbeds Support can reset a property user's password from the Users page, cannot grant credit-card viewing, and sends lock-outs to a security team ([Cloudbeds: users and activity log](https://myfrontdesk.cloudbeds.com/hc//articles/221793248)). HospAI's shipped design goes further than anything either incumbent advertises. Operators sign in through a dedicated Clerk instance with a required second factor, the console Worker has a single binding into `pms-core`, an operator actor passes no tenant permission check, and the hotel reads every account change under "HospAI account changes" ([ops platform spec](../../../docs/superpowers/specs/2026-11-22-ops-platform-design.md); [ARCHITECTURE.md §4.9](../../../ARCHITECTURE.md)). There is no impersonation ("log in as" a customer user) anywhere in the code.

**General practice says the costly parts are already done.** Microsoft's guidance is that under about ten tenants a full control plane may be unnecessary, provided there is still "a central location to track your tenants" and documented procedures ([Azure: control planes](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/multitenant/considerations/control-planes)). HospAI is at that scale and already has more than the guidance asks for. The items the standards test, and that are hard to add later, are individual operator identity, a second factor, a reason on each action and an append-only log keyed by tenant. PCI DSS v4.0.1 requires logs of "all actions taken by any individual with administrative access" (10.2.1.2), protected from modification (10.3.2) and kept twelve months (10.5.1) ([PCI DSS v4.0.1](https://www.middlebury.edu/sites/default/files/2025-01/PCI-DSS-v4_0_1.pdf)). HospAI's `operator_action` table blocks UPDATE and DELETE with triggers and stores a reason on every row. Two fields in the PCI list are not among its columns: where the action came from, and whether it succeeded or failed (10.2.2). (Inference) Adding origin and recording refused attempts is a small change now and a migration later.

**The separate app is the pattern the security sources recommend.** Mews and Oracle put operator tooling inside the main product behind permissions (the Mews Onboarding dashboard, Oracle's Toolbox menu), which is a deliberate design choice ([Mews Onboarding System Audit](https://help.mews.com/s/article/Mews-Onboarding-System-Audit); [Oracle: Refresh Property](https://docs.oracle.com/en/industries/hospitality/opera-cloud/25.1/ocsuh/t_toolbox_re-provisioning_refresh_property.htm)). Azure, AWS and the security vendor Aikido all argue for a separately deployed admin application that calls private control-plane APIs, because a control plane compromise reaches every tenant ([Azure: control planes](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/multitenant/considerations/control-planes); [Aikido](https://www.aikido.dev/blog/build-secure-admin-panel)). HospAI took that route. The same sources explain why a low-code admin tool wired straight to the database would have been wrong for a PMS: it bypasses the money and inventory rules the product enforces (inference from the build-versus-buy sources, all of which are vendor-written).

**Copy setup looks like a differentiator, on thin evidence.** The research found no Cloudbeds or Mews article describing "copy this property's configuration to a new property"; Oracle's export and import of "configuration shells" and Stayntouch's Excel Auto-Configurator are the only documented equivalents ([Stayntouch onboarding outline](https://stayntouch.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/24000013054-stayntouch-pms-onboarding-detailed-outline)). HospAI's `ops.copy_setup` already does this for a group rollout. The limit of the claim is that absence from help centres does not prove the incumbents lack an internal tool.

**Two cautions about the shipped version.** The console postdates both codebase audits, has had at most a day of staging use, and had two defects found and fixed on the day it shipped. Its own threat surface (first-admin bootstrap from an environment variable, linking by verified email, trust in the operator id the console Worker passes) has spec-level tests but no independent review on record. And one path deserves a second look: a single `ops_admin` can make any signed-up person an owner of any customer through `ops.add_org_owner`, with no second approver. The action is logged and the hotel can see it, but it is an indirect route to tenant data that the stricter grant model was designed to close. Both points are the inventory researcher's reading of the code, not statements found in the docs.

## What is missing, in the order to consider it

### First, with no new slice: deploy the alerts, write the kill-switch procedure, rehearse a restore

**Alerts come before everything else.** AWS's guidance is to "configure alerts and alarms that are triggered when tenants reach specific health states" ([AWS SaaS Lens](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/saas-lens/tenant-aware-operations.html)). HospAI's R7.2 sweep has nine signals, including a halted or overdue night audit. It already runs on staging on a 5-minute cron: by 2026-11-25 it had opened and resolved 7 overdue-audit alerts and 10 unreachable-hotel alerts, none of which notified anyone, because no paging sink is set. The slice is still unticked, and Track G places it before the first hotel while the slices placed after it have already shipped ([roadmap](../../../docs/roadmap.md)). Finishing it means the account set-up in its spec, a paging vendor and sink, an off-Cloudflare status page and a production deploy. Two platform facts make the sweep more valuable than it looks. A Durable Object alarm (the timer each per-hotel object uses to schedule work) is retried six times and then does not run again until code re-arms it, so a night audit can stop silently ([Cloudflare: alarms](https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/api/alarms/)). And a dead-letter queue (where messages go after repeated failure) with no consumer deletes its messages after four days ([Cloudflare: dead letter queues](https://developers.cloudflare.com/queues/configuration/dead-letter-queues/)).

**The kill switch needs a written procedure.** A kill switch is a global off-switch for one feature. HospAI's is a key in Cloudflare KV, and the only code that sets it is a test helper, so in a deployed environment the switch can only be thrown by a direct KV write outside the application, unrecorded. No runbook states the steps. Vendor guidance on kill switches is consistent that each toggle should be audited and should alert (search summaries of [Datadog](https://docs.datadoghq.com/feature_flags/use_cases/kill_switches/) and [Statsig](https://statsig.com/perspectives/kill-switches-in-software-development)). Until an audited command exists, a one-page procedure naming who may write the key, how, and where it is recorded closes most of the risk.

**The restore drill has more steps than the earlier research assumed.** Point-in-time recovery (PITR) lets a Durable Object's database be rewound to any moment in the last 30 days, but only by code running inside that object, and it does not work in local development, so the drill must run on staging ([Cloudflare: SQLite storage API](https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/api/sqlite-storage-api/)). The restore call returns an "undo" bookmark; that and the reason must be stored outside the restored object, because anything written inside it, including its own audit log, is rewound too. (Inference) A restored hotel is then behind everything derived from it: events already delivered, documents in R2, the organisation's guest vault and any payment or filing made since. A restore to a point before a guest erasure brings the erased data back, so erasures made since the bookmark must be re-applied from a ledger held elsewhere.

**The console itself is not yet live.** Staging is complete: the repository secret was set and the first operator signed in on 2026-11-24. The hostname and a production Clerk instance are still open ([web-admin README](../../../apps/web-admin/README.md)). (Inference) Given the two cautions above, a review of the console's own threat surface belongs before it gets a production hostname.

### Second, inside the R7.3 spec: the operations half

R7.3 is "direction only; write its own spec first", and it follows R7.4 because its fleet columns come from the projection R7.4 builds. Its direction already holds four things the research supports: support access grants, fleet health columns, deployed version and schema version per property, and a weekly access review. The research adds detail to each and suggests a few neighbours to weigh when the spec is written.

**Support grants should behave as the research says impersonation must.** The converging pattern is a mandatory reason, a hard limit of 30 to 60 minutes, a marker in the session, a banner, and start and end events in the log; GitHub also emails the affected user and records the session in that user's own security log ([WorkOS: impersonation](https://workos.com/docs/user-management/impersonation); [GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/enterprise-server@3.17/admin/managing-accounts-and-repositories/managing-users-in-your-enterprise/impersonating-a-user)). HospAI's planned limits (read up to 60 minutes, write up to 15 with a second approver) sit inside that range, and its choice to have the operator act as an operator, never as a hotel user, avoids the side effects WorkOS lists for impersonation: guest-facing messages, last-login timestamps, and background jobs that run later under the wrong name ([WorkOS blog](https://workos.com/blog/user-impersonation-side-effects)). (Inference) For a PMS the equivalents are guest messages, cashier-shift attribution and night-audit jobs, and the grant should also mask card data and identity documents. Enterprise vendors go one step further and let the customer grant access for a period it chooses, as Databricks does for up to 48 hours ([Databricks](https://docs.databricks.com/aws/en/admin/workspace/workspace-access)). HospAI's interim rule, where the hotel adds a named HospAI member, is already a crude form of that.

**Fleet health should include the alert list and a night-audit board.** Today nothing but the sweep reads the alert table, so even with the sweep running the console cannot show what is open. (Inference) A night-audit board across hotels (business date, local time, state, time in state) is the hotel-specific view with the best case behind it. A stuck audit blocks the next day's charges and arrivals list, yet the Mews service-level agreement would class it only as "high priority", with an 8-hour standard response, because its "critical" level covers the whole platform being down ([Mews SLA](https://www.mews.com/en/legal/service-level-agreement)). The only detailed public list of audit failures is Oracle's page for legacy OPERA 5, where support fixes a hung posting job by editing its status in the database and is warned that a wrong edit "can jeopardize the property's financial data" ([OPERA End of Day troubleshooting](https://docs.oracle.com/cd/E98457_01/opera_5_6_core_help/end_of_day_troubleshooting.htm)). No cloud vendor documents its replacement for that.

**Version tracking needs a ledger the platform does not provide.** Each Durable Object migrates its own private database when it wakes, and Cloudflare offers no fleet view of schema versions; its object-listing API returns unnamed ids only ([Cloudflare: list objects](https://developers.cloudflare.com/api/resources/durable_objects/subresources/namespaces/subresources/objects/index.md)). Microsoft states the requirement directly: the control plane must know "which version of infrastructure, software, or feature each tenant uses" ([Azure: updates](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/multitenant/considerations/updates)). HospAI has a migration sweep, but its trigger is registered only in development and its results go to logs. (Inference) A dormant hotel stays on an old schema until something wakes it, so the sweep needs a production trigger and a results table before old migration code can ever be removed.

**Neighbours worth weighing in the same spec.** An audited command to set and clear kill switches, with a list of every switch that is on. The routine user fixes that practitioner sources put first, since support that cannot resend an invitation or remove a leaver ends up in the identity provider's dashboard, outside the operator log. Showing who a customer's owners are (the console shows user ids only) and searching by hotel name. The owner invitation left open as spec question Q3. Origin and outcome on the action log. A decision on whether adding an owner to a customer that already has one needs more than one operator.

### Third, before about ten hotels: five tools that each need their own spec

**Practice mode.** Apaleo creates every property in a Test status in which transactions can be wiped while setup is kept ([Apaleo help centre](https://apaleo.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/4403408049554), search summary; the page refused the researcher's fetch), Oracle's Refresh Property re-provisions a non-production property from an exported configuration, and Stayntouch gives staff a training environment in weeks 5 to 7 of onboarding ([Oracle: Refresh Property](https://docs.oracle.com/en/industries/hospitality/opera-cloud/25.1/ocsuh/t_toolbox_re-provisioning_refresh_property.htm); [Stayntouch outline](https://stayntouch.freshdesk.com/support/solutions/articles/24000013054-stayntouch-pms-onboarding-detailed-outline)). Front-desk turnover is put at 50 to 80% a year by one vendor's marketing ([Stayntouch](https://www.stayntouch.com/articles/?p=107), search summary), so training does not end at go-live. HospAI has no practice mode; its demo seed refuses to run outside local development.

**Tenant export and a view of purge certificates.** Neither incumbent publishes an export format or a retrieval window. Azure's advice is to keep a distinct deactivated state for "reactivation, export, dispute resolution, or a retention period" and to keep audit records after tenant data is deleted ([Azure: tenant life cycle](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/multitenant/considerations/tenant-life-cycle)). HospAI has the purge workflow and certificates but no export, and the console cannot read the certificates. Cloudflare has no export or clone API for Durable Objects, so export is application work.

**An onboarding record.** Mews gates go-live on an audit that mixes configuration checks, items flagged for a human to review and commercial conditions such as settled invoices, and records GO or NO GO; Cloudbeds runs seven stages ending in a 10-day hypercare with check-ins on days 2 and 9 ([Mews Onboarding System Audit](https://help.mews.com/s/article/Mews-Onboarding-System-Audit); [Cloudbeds onboarding](https://myfrontdesk.cloudbeds.com/hc/en-us/articles/360041220954-What-to-expect-during-Cloudbeds-onboarding)). Hypercare is the period of intensified support just after go-live. HospAI's "adding a hotel" steps are a checklist in the roadmap. (Inference) A go-live date and a "hypercare until" date on the customer record are the smallest useful version, because they tell support and alerting which hotels are fragile.

**Incident notices and announcements.** Mews computes service credits from its public status page, which makes the page a contractual ledger, and uses in-app pop-ups and banners; real incidents are often partial ("some properties"), so the vendor needs an internal list of who was affected ([Mews SLA](https://www.mews.com/en/legal/service-level-agreement); [status.mews.com](https://status.mews.com/)). HospAI scoped a hotel-facing incident banner out of R7.2 and has no announcement mechanism. (Inference) For Indian pilot hotels a message to the on-duty contact by a channel that does not depend on the PMS matters more than a status page; no source measures this.

**Queue inspection and replay.** Mews exposes a per-property channel-manager queue where a failed item can be retried, and its incident notes describe bulk re-requesting of bookings ([Mews channel manager queue](https://help.mews.com/s/article/channel-manager-queue), search summary). Cloudflare Queues offer counts and pause, with no way to browse messages by tenant and no built-in redrive ([Cloudflare: Queues metrics](https://developers.cloudflare.com/queues/observability/metrics/)). HospAI's hotel-side exceptions screen already has retry and dismiss; the console has neither a list nor a replay. (Inference) Any replay must go back through the normal consumer so permission, entitlement and idempotency checks still apply.

Release rings belong in this group once there are enough hotels to stage a rollout. Cloudflare's gradual deployments split traffic by percentage and bind each Durable Object to one version, but the operator cannot choose which hotels move first, so a ring has to be a per-tenant setting evaluated in code ([Cloudflare: gradual deployments with Durable Objects](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/versions-and-deployments/gradual-deployments/with-durable-objects/)).

### Later, each on a named trigger

Several console areas the research describes have no trigger yet in HospAI's plan, and building them early would be the over-building that practitioner sources warn about. Channel and integration health waits for the channel-manager connector (R4.3); the external clock to design for then is Booking.com's fallback, which emails the hotel directly if the provider has not fetched a reservation within 30 minutes (search summary of [Booking.com's FAQ](https://developers.booking.com/connectivity/docs/con-faq-reservations-missing-res-messages)). A payments compliance queue (know-your-customer checks on the hotel's legal entity, which at Cloudbeds can disable a merchant account) waits for a payment provider; UPI in HospAI is a tender recorded at the desk. A device registry waits for the first lock or telephone interface. A dated ownership-transfer workflow waits for the first sale of a hotel; one vendor's stated experience is that a fresh account for the new legal entity, with the old one kept read-only for audits, is the cleaner route ([Clock PMS](https://support.clock-software.com/en/support/solutions/articles/9000269223-changing-legal-entities-considerations-you-can-t-overlook)). A seasonal dormant state waits for the first hill or beach property that closes for months. Usage metering waits for a usage-priced add-on, fleet reporting for the warehouse, and support notes and validated ticket links for a chosen helpdesk.

## India adds dated duties, on secondary evidence

Every fact in this section comes from law-firm, tax-portal, press or vendor summaries. None was read in the notification or Rules text. Check each against the primary source, with an advisor, before building on it.

The reported changes matter to the console because they arrive with little notice and apply to every hotel at once. Hotel room GST is reported to have changed from 22 September 2025, to 5% without input tax credit for rooms up to ₹7,500 a night and 18% above, after a Council meeting on 3 September and a notification reported as dated 17 September ([ClearTax](https://cleartax.in/s/impact-of-gst-hospitality-industry); [Petpooja](https://blog.petpooja.com/?p=47409)). Sources conflict on whether rooms up to ₹1,000 are exempt, and the researchers could not settle it. The restaurant test for "specified premises" is reported to have changed from 1 April 2025 ([Taxmann](https://www.taxmann.com/post/blog/cbic-issued-faqs-to-explain-criteria-procedures-and-gst-implications-for-restaurant-services-supplied-at-specified-premises/)). Foreign-guest reporting is reported to have moved from Form C to an electronic Form III, due within 24 hours of arrival, under Rules in force from 1 September 2025 ([Morung Express](https://morungexpress.com/dimapur-police-issues-directive-on-foreigners-registration-compliance)); HospAI's tracker and its night-audit check `form_c_overdue` still carry the old name. The data-protection Rules were notified in November 2025 with the substantive duties, including a 72-hour breach report, reported to start on 13 May 2027 ([Hogan Lovells](https://www.hoganlovells.com/en/publications/indias-digital-personal-data-protection-act-2023-brought-into-force-); [EY](https://www.ey.com/en_in/insights/cybersecurity/transforming-data-privacy-digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025)). For billing hotels later, recurring card charges above ₹15,000 are reported to need the cardholder's extra authentication each time ([Business Today](https://www.businesstoday.in/personal-finance/news/story/rbi-caps-recurring-payments-at-rs15000-without-otp-under-new-e-mandate-framework-526759-2026-04-21)).

(Inference) Two console needs follow if these hold. Nineteen days from announcement to effect is too short to rely on each hotel editing its own tax rules, which points to rule sets published centrally with an effective date and a per-hotel report of who is on which version. And a vendor-side list of foreign arrivals with no submission after some hours would let support warn hotels before the deadline. The first collides with a locked decision and appears below as a question. The second fits the fleet view without one, because it is a count and carries no guest data.

## Seven locked decisions the research presses on

Each of these is recorded as decided in the ops spec, the roadmap or AGENTS.md. The research does not justify overturning any of them. It does raise a question on each that only the owner can answer.

**Cancel and purge stay owner-only tenant routes.** Azure describes cancellation and offboarding as control-plane work, and both incumbents can terminate for non-payment. Under HospAI's rule a suspended customer that never cancels stays suspended indefinitely. Question: what is the procedure for an abandoned or unreachable customer, and should the console at least show the lifecycle state and purge certificates read-only?

**Break-glass SQL is outside the platform.** Cloudflare's Data Studio can read and write one object's database from the dashboard and logs each query in Cloudflare's audit log, not in HospAI's operator log ([Cloudflare: Data Studio](https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/observability/data-studio/)). ARCHITECTURE.md says break-glass use "automatically opens an incident record"; no implementation exists. Question: who holds the Cloudflare role that allows it, and is a manual entry in the operator log required after each use?

**Data import (R4.8) is deferred.** Every documented vendor runs an assisted import with a trial run about a week before cut-over, and none documents a rollback. The roadmap already carries the open decision on whether to build the first stage before hotel 2. Question: unchanged, with one addition from the research, that imported rows carry a batch id so a bad batch can be reversed before the hotel starts trading (an inference, not a documented practice).

**Billing is R5.6 and out of the console.** The Mews go-live audit checks that subscription invoices are settled, and both contracts tie suspension to overdue invoices, which is why general practice wants payment status visible beside tenant status. Question: until R5.6, should the customer record carry a manually kept paid-through date, or does billing stay wholly outside?

**`entitlement_override` and trial grants are not built.** General sources list per-tenant overrides and time-boxed grants as standard. HospAI's rule is that every concession is a plan or an add-on. Question: is there a real case, such as a hotel trialling an add-on for a month, that an add-on quantity with a manual removal date does not cover?

**The console does not edit a live hotel's configuration.** Central tax rollout would be exactly such an edit, and under ARCHITECTURE.md §4.9 a write into a live hotel needs a second approver. Question: when the next GST change lands, does each hotel's accountant change its own rules by the effective date, or does HospAI publish a rule set, and through which control?

**Dual control is not weakened for a solo operator.** The research notes that four-eyes approval is impractical with one or two operators and found no sourced substitute. Spec question Q2 (who the second operator is, and when) already covers this. Question: does the single-operator `ops.add_org_owner` path need the same answer?

The roadmap queue stays serial. Nothing here proposes running slices in parallel; the order above is an order of consideration within that queue.

## Three inconsistencies the inventory found in the repository

**Stale roadmap wording for R7.1.** The R7.1 heading says the console shipped with its "own Clerk instance per spec §15", but its Locked list still says the Worker sits "behind Cloudflare Access with a hardware key" and "verifies the Access JWT on every request", and its Tests list still says a bad or missing Access JWT returns 401 ([roadmap, R7.1](../../../docs/roadmap.md)). The spec amendment and the code show Clerk is what shipped; Cloudflare Access is an optional extra layer. This is a documentation defect only.

**Kill-switch cache time stated three ways.** ARCHITECTURE.md §6.4 and the entitlements spec say the switch is read with a 10-second cache. The code reads it with a 60-second cache ([entitlement-client](../../../packages/entitlement-client/src/index.ts)). The ops spec's build note says KV's minimum cache time is 30 seconds, so the documented 10 seconds cannot be configured at all, and reads `kill:ops.write` uncached. The practical meaning is that a thrown feature kill switch can take up to a minute to bite, not ten seconds.

**A kill switch with no control.** Both the per-feature switch and `kill:ops.write` exist as mechanisms, but no console screen, command or runbook sets them; the only setter in code is a helper marked test-only. The switch that ARCHITECTURE.md says can void every outstanding support grant is therefore operable only by a direct, unaudited KV write.

Two smaller mismatches sit beside these. ARCHITECTURE.md §4.9 says a ticket reference is "required, validated against the helpdesk system", while the shipped field is optional free text and no helpdesk integration exists. And R7.2, which Track G places before the first hotel, is still unticked, runs on staging without paging and is not in production, while the slices placed after it have shipped.

## Conclusion

The useful way to read the first console is as the half that could not be done by runbook. Creating customers and changing plans by hand-written SQL left no record; that is fixed, and fixed to a standard the incumbents do not publicly meet. What remains can mostly start as procedure and become software when it repeats, which is what Microsoft advises at this scale and what Google's reliability writing means by engineering away work that grows with the number of tenants ([Google SRE book](https://sre.google/sre-book/eliminating-toil/)). The exception is alerting. A night audit that stops at 2 a.m. with nobody paged is the one failure a runbook cannot catch, and the code to catch it already runs on staging; what it lacks is someone to page.

The less obvious result is where HospAI's strictness will be tested. The design refuses standing access, impersonation, console edits of live hotels and raw SQL. Those refusals hold easily while one owner runs ten hotels and can add a HospAI member on request. They come under pressure at the first tax change with a two-week deadline, the first stuck audit at a hotel whose owner is asleep, and the first customer who stops paying and stops answering. The seven questions above are those three situations in advance. Answering them while writing the R7.3 spec costs a paragraph each; answering them during the incident costs a break-glass write that the platform, by design, will not record.
